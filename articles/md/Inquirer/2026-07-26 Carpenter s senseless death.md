### Editorial

# Carpenter’s senseless death

#### Philippine Daily Inquirer / 05:15 AM July 26, 2026

[https://opinion.inquirer.net/193337/carpenters-senseless-death](https://opinion.inquirer.net/193337/carpenters-senseless-death)

---

The Philippines has lost an ally in marine biodiversity conservation and protection, and the fight for territorial rights in the West Philippine Sea (WPS) with the death of renowned American marine biologist Dr. Kent Carpenter.

His senseless death after a violent invasion of his home in Sibulan, Negros Oriental, makes it even more tragic.

Carpenter first arrived in the Philippines in 1975, when he was 22, as a member of the United States Peace Corps. He had just earned his degree in marine biology from the Florida Institute of Technology and was assigned to the Bureau of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources to conduct coral reef research. This would eventually set the direction of his career, calling the assignment “the best job there ever was or ever will be in the Peace Corps.”

In 1976, he became affiliated with Siliman University, where he would later be appointed as a faculty member. In 1985, he earned his doctorate in zoology from the University of Hawaii. In 1996, he joined Old Dominion University in Norfolk, Virginia, as a professor of biological sciences.

## Groundbreaking contributions

In a statement last week, the Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) paid tribute to Carpenter by recognizing his decades of research that “helped deepen scientific understanding and advance conservation efforts.” “Dr. Kent’s contributions were instrumental in strengthening the scientific foundation for marine biodiversity conservation in the country,” Environment Secretary Juan Miguel Cuna said.

Two of Carpenter’s most important and groundbreaking contributions over a five-decade career reflect a legacy that has crossed from the academe to international law: his research on the Verde Island Passage (VIP) and his expert testimony to the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) on the Philippines’ territorial claims case against China. The latter has given the country a final and binding ruling recognizing its maritime rights over the WPS and the harm done to shared marine resources, an invaluable public service that will matter for generations to come.

In 2005, Carpenter’s study called VIP the “center of the center” of marine shore-fish biodiversity, highlighting the significance of the narrow passage between Luzon and Mindoro. VIP hosts the highest concentration of shorefish species on the planet, with over 1,736 overlapping species coexisting within just a 10-square-kilometer area. He has supported a campaign launched in September 2024 to declare VIP as a World Heritage Site.

## Expert witness

He also conducted pioneering studies on the Tubbataha Reefs and other Philippine marine ecosystems, which helped demonstrate their exceptional ecological value and reinforced the significance of Tubbataha Reefs Natural Park as a Unesco World Heritage Site, according to the DENR.

His expertise on marine biodiversity qualified him to serve as an expert witness in the landmark South China Sea Arbitration (Republic of the Philippines v. People’s Republic of China) before the PCA in The Hague. His testimonies, contained in the “First Carpenter Report” (March 2014), “Second Carpenter Report” (November 2015), and “Third Carpenter Report” (2016), as well as his oral testimony in November 2015, provided scientific backbone for the legal arguments on the impact of China’s large-scale dredging, artificial island construction, and destructive giant clam harvesting across Spratly Islands, the Paracel Islands, and Scarborough Shoal.

In making its 2016 ruling, the tribunal relied on these reports to conclude that China caused irreparable harm, and they remain an important documentation amid Beijing’s refusal to acknowledge the arbitral ruling and the continuous harassment that Filipino fishermen and the Philippine Navy experience in the WPS.

## Heinous crime

Carpenter’s extraordinary life was ended on July 12 when three men broke into his house while two others acted as lookouts. The marine biologist, who was watching television with his Filipino partner, was shot twice in the head, causing instantaneous death. He was 73.

Last week, the Philippine National Police declared Carpenter’s killing as “case solved” after the sixth suspect was identified. The suspects took a laptop and cash. Four of them are now under police custody, while two remain at large. They face charges of robbery with homicide, and the PNP has dismissed any work-related motive in the killing. Nonetheless, his death should serve as a reminder of the threats that scientists, researchers, and environmental defenders like him have faced and continue to face.

Authorities must ensure that those behind Carpenter’s death are brought to justice and pay for their heinous crime. No one deserves such a violent death, much more someone who has dedicated his career and expertise to the service of the Philippines, a country that was not even his own.
